Day 27 · Phase D

Silence isn't an answer
It's a blank, and you filled it in with the ugliest reading available

The information content of silence · a re-open they can answer in one second · triaging dead deals by trigger · getting the loss reason back on your way out·4 moves · 4 diagrams
Their silence is probably not about you. But the script you wrote to explain it will make you send the worst message of the whole relationship.
"No reply" is an answer in your head; on their side it's just something that hasn't come up yet. One silence fits six or seven different causes, and the right response to each contradicts the others — so its information content is close to zero. What kills these deals isn't the silence, it's that you can't tolerate the blank: you fill it with the most self-relevant reading available (too expensive, they're sick of me) and then send the next message from that script — either pushing harder or vanishing in embarrassment. There is exactly one correct move: stop decoding the silence and drive the cost of replying down, low enough that one character is worth their while.
MOVE 01

Don't decode silence: there is no code, you just picked the reading that hurts most A default is not a signal

default ≠ signalspotlight effectattribution bias
The principle in one line
Silence isn't a no and it isn't a yes. It's a default — nothing happened. Read a non-event as an event and you'll take an expensive action on zero information: double the pestering, or disappear entirely. What those two have in common is that you're negotiating with your own imagination, and imagination never comes down on price.
One silence, six unrelated situations
In the room
Situation: the proposal went out eleven days ago. Not a word back.
✗ Writing from the script in your head

"Is there something in the proposal that doesn't work? If budget is tight we could look at that." You've walked "they think it's expensive" — a cause you invented — right up to them, and discounted on the way. If the real reason was that finance hadn't signed off, you just gave away a round of margin for nothing, and taught them something costly: stay quiet and the price falls on its own.

✓ Admit you don't know; hand the decoding back

"I've not heard anything, and I can't tell whether it's not a priority, stuck internally, or the direction changed — what I should be doing is completely different in each case. Reply with a number: 1 not a priority / 2 stuck internally / 3 dead."
—— No decoding, no invented cause, no self-inflicted discount. The cost of telling you which box they're in is now one keystroke.

Why it works · mechanism and evidence
Why it works

Mechanism: the mind won't tolerate a blank, so it fills it with the most available explanation — and the most available one for you is always the one that involves you. You overestimate how much room you occupy in their head, and you overestimate how your last message landed. Stack the two and silence gets auto-translated into "they have a problem with me".

  • Hard evidence · you're less noticed than you think: the spotlight effect, Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky (2000, JPSP). Participants walking into a room in an embarrassing T-shirt predicted about 46% of observers could name who was on it; the real figure was about 23% — off by a factor of two. Peer-reviewed, repeatedly replicated. Implication: the message you keep re-reading has most likely not been thought about once.
  • Hard evidence · tone distorts in text: Kruger, Epley, Parker & Ng (2005, JPSP), Egocentrism over e-mail. Senders predicted recipients would detect whether they were serious or sarcastic about 78% of the time; the actual rate was around 56%, barely above a coin flip. Peer-reviewed experiment. It cuts both ways: your last message may not have read as well as you think, and their silence may not be as cold as you read it.

Add a layer of Ross's (1977) fundamental attribution error and you read circumstance as character: the likeliest reason is "seventeen more urgent things today", and you hear "they don't want to deal with me". That said, silence sometimes is a signal, and the test is baseline: someone who always replied within ten minutes going quiet past a date they set is a change; someone who always took three days taking five is nothing at all. And don't read any of this as "never reflect" — if your silent list gets longer every year, what you're selling simply isn't important to them.

Useful phrasing
Say it like this

"I'd rather ask than assume."

"Reply with a number if that's easier — 1, 2 or 3."

"No news is genuinely no news."

MOVE 02

The job of a re-open isn't to get an answer — it's to make replying cost one word You can only move the cost side

cost of replyingone-word exitfreedom to refuse
The principle in one line
They aren't silent because they don't want to reply. They're silent because replying costs more right now than it returns: recall which thread this is, ask around internally, take a position, then find a tone that doesn't offend. You can't remove any of those four for them — but you can rewrite the message so a reply is one digit, and the change in response rate will make you wonder what the last six months were for.
In their words
"No is the start of the negotiation, not the end of it." — Chris Voss, Never Split the Difference (2016)
Four tiers, ranked by what it costs them
Scripts: four tiers, cheapest first
Every tier carries its own exit — the exit is why it's cheap

· Tier 1 · the one-word exit. "Should I take this off the list for this year? If so, just reply 'not this year' and I'll stop — no explanation needed, and it's a perfectly reasonable call." It converts "reject you" into "do you a favour", and says out loud that refusing is allowed.

· Tier 2 · reply with a number. "Just a number: 1 not a priority / 2 stuck internally / 3 direction changed." The options must include the one that's worst for you — leave it out and it's a leading question, which anyone can spot.

· Tier 3 · bring something, waive the reply. "I put the new rules in your sector onto one page. Attached, no reply needed." It isn't there to get an answer; it's there to keep your record at "opening this pays".

· Tier 4 · ask for status outright. "Where is this now?" The expensive one. Save it until you've got any signal at all. Opening a silence at tier 4 is asking someone who owes you money for another loan.

In the room
Situation: a customer you got on well with, one you thought was safe, has gone quiet for two months.
✗ The quieter it gets, the harder you push

"Are you around?" → "Got two minutes?" → "Just wondering whether this is still moving on your side?" Three messages, three demands for a paragraph, each one reminding them: you owe me a reply. People in debt hide. That's ordinary human behaviour, not a character flaw. Every message you add makes the debt heavier and hiding cheaper.

✓ Tier 1, with the right to refuse handed over explicitly

"I've left you alone for two months. Should I take this off the list for this year? If so, just reply 'not this year' and I'll stop — no explanation needed, it's a reasonable call. If it's only a priority thing, I'll knock again around budget season."
—— If they say "not this year", you've saved six months of guessing. If they say "it's not dead, it's stuck internally", you've got the deal back. Both beat guessing — and that's exactly why it works: you genuinely accept either.

Why it works · mechanism and evidence
Why it works

Two layers. The cost side: reply rate isn't set by how much they want to help you, it's set by how much effort the reply takes — and effort is the only side you control. And autonomy: saying "you're completely free to refuse" defuses reactance. People being pushed instinctively brace against it; people explicitly handed an exit are the ones willing to stay and say something. Which is why the sentence most likely to get a reply is the one that offers them a graceful way out.

  • Hard evidence · affirming the freedom to refuse raises compliance: Carpenter (2013, Communication Studies), a meta-analysis of 42 studies and over twenty thousand participants on the but-you-are-free technique: compliance rises significantly (r ≈ .13). Peer-reviewed meta-analysis. Boundary: the effect is strongest face-to-face and delivered right after the request, and smaller in written or delayed settings — in email it's a bonus, not magic.
  • Hard evidence · choice is itself motivating: Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008, Psychological Bulletin), a meta-analysis of 41 studies: giving people a choice significantly raises intrinsic motivation and effort (d ≈ 0.30). Peer-reviewed. So "1 / 2 / 3" isn't only convenient; it converts being interrogated into making a choice.
  • Practitioner lore · the no-oriented question: Voss's "Have you given up on this project?" comes from negotiation practice, and no peer-reviewed experiment has tested that sentence directly. Mechanically it's the same family as the two above (replying only requires denying an unflattering claim, not committing to anything), but the evidence grade is lore, not law.

The one-word exit has to be a real exit. Keep sending after they say "not this year" and the sentence is dead for good; and what you've spent isn't one deal, it's the credibility of everything you say. One more boundary: these four tiers lower the cost of replying, not the cost of deciding. When they answer "2, stuck internally", the actual work begins — that's what the decision-chain and pain-digging pieces are for. A re-open only turns the lights on.

Useful phrasing
Say it like this

"Should I take this off my list for this year?"

"A one-word yes is completely fine — and a reasonable call."

"Of course, you're free to say no."

MOVE 03

Most of your dead deals didn't lose to a rival. They lost to doing nothing. The lever is what changed on their side

status quo biastrigger eventstriage
The principle in one line
The lever for reviving a dead deal is never your enthusiasm; it's what changed on their side. Inaction is the default, and changing costs decision effort, migration pain, and the risk of being the person who signed off on a mistake. The scales only move when the status quo starts hurting or the outside conditions flip. No trigger, no knock — otherwise you're just collecting the same no a second time, and harder.
In their words
"Most real decisions, unlike those of economics texts, have a status quo alternative — that is, doing nothing or maintaining one's current or previous decision." — Samuelson & Zeckhauser, Status Quo Bias in Decision Making (1988)
Triage: four ways they die, four different switches
Script: the three-line revival message
All three lines are about them; none is about you

· Line one: name the trigger. "Saw you folded the east region in last month —" rather than "it's been a while —". The first happened in their world, the second in yours, and only the first gets a reaction.

· Line two: quote them back to themselves. "You said two people could still handle reconciliation manually." That proves what you remembered was their problem, not your product. The whole weight of the line is in being accurate: misremembering is worse than not remembering.

· Line three: a cheap question plus an exit. "Is that still true? If it's not relevant, say so and I'll drop it off the reminder list."

Why it works · mechanism and evidence
Why it works

Mechanism: inaction is a default with gravity. Change costs decision effort and migration effort, and carries the "what if I was the one who signed off on this" exposure; when nothing changes and it goes wrong, the blame is diffuse. Add that losses weigh more than equivalent gains and the scales sit naturally on staying put. What actually moves them isn't the force of your persuasion, it's a change on their side.

  • Hard evidence · status quo bias: Samuelson & Zeckhauser (1988, Journal of Risk and Uncertainty): a series of experiments plus real pension and health-plan election data, all showing the default option is chosen far more often than it should be. Replicated across domains — the starkest being the organ-donation comparison in Johnson & Goldstein (2003, Science): countries whose default is "enrolled unless you opt out" run consent above ninety percent, while opt-in countries sit in the single and low double digits.
  • Hard evidence (neural) · why losses pin people in place: Tom, Fox, Trepel & Poldrack (2007, Science) scanned gambling decisions and found activity in the ventral striatum and ventromedial prefrontal cortex rising with potential gains and falling with potential losses, with a steeper slope on the loss side; an individual's neural steepness even predicted their behavioural loss aversion. Honest label: these are laboratory money gambles, and carrying them into corporate purchasing is mechanism extrapolation. Also De Martino, Camerer & Adolphs (2010, PNAS): two patients with amygdala damage showed no behavioural loss aversion — a strong mechanistic clue, but n=2, suggestive rather than statistical.

There's also omission bias (Ritov & Baron, 1990): the same bad outcome is harder to accept when caused by doing something than by doing nothing — buying the wrong thing gets you named, not buying just doesn't happen. As for "trigger", it means a verifiable, objective change on their side: people moved, a merger, funding, a new system, an incident, a budget cycle landing. "It's been three months since we spoke" is not a trigger; that's your calendar going off. And a harsher self-check: if a deal has stalled three times on the same person and you never once met whoever pays, it isn't a dead deal — it was never alive.

Useful phrasing
Say it like this

"Saw you merged the east-region team last month —"

"You said two people could still handle it manually. Is that still true?"

"If it's not relevant, say so and I'll drop it."

MOVE 04

The moment you let go is the only moment you get the truth Ask for a letter, not an explanation

self-serving biassurvivorshipone-tap loss reason
The principle in one line
The post-mortem you write yourself isn't trustworthy. Self-serving attribution is systematic: wins are your playbook, losses are the price, the timing, the unprofessional customer. What those three have in common is that none of them requires you to change anything. So you keep choosing them, and keep losing the same kind of deal.
Letting go: three steps, three things back
Script: the closing note plus a one-tap reason
One note doing three jobs: close it, hold the door, take the intelligence

"I'm closing this out on my side, and you don't owe me an explanation. If you're willing to do me one small favour: just reply with a letter. A timing / B went with someone else / C no internal sign-off / D we didn't make the case. That's worth more to me than a win. The mapping sheet is yours whenever you want it, no strings."

· Why ask only after letting go. While you still want the deal, what you get is a socially acceptable answer — "too expensive" is the cheapest refusal available and usually isn't the real one. Once you've said you're done, they no longer have to protect your feelings or their negotiating position. Practitioner lore, no experimental support.

· Never ask "why didn't you pick us". That invites them to invent a reason on the spot. Ask what they actually did: "What did you end up doing instead — or is it still as it was?" Behaviour is far more reliable than reasons, which is the core of the Mom Test piece.

· A letter, not an essay. Raise the cost of replying and you get nothing at all. Ten letters in, you'll see a pattern you've been refusing to admit.

Why it works · mechanism and evidence
Why it works

Two layers. Attribution: the loss reason you write gets pushed toward external causes, so the more diligently you review, the more consistently you're wrong. Sampling: you only draw lessons from deals that closed, so the sample is truncated on the surviving side and the pattern you extract has to be skewed. Neither hole can be fixed by thinking harder — only by buying a cheap, honest category from outside.

  • Hard evidence · self-serving attribution: Mezulis, Abramson, Hyde & Hankin (2004, Psychological Bulletin), a meta-analysis of 266 studies: crediting success internally and failure externally is robust in adult samples, with systematic variation by age and culture.
  • Hard evidence (statistical) · survivorship bias: Abraham Wald and Columbia's Statistical Research Group (1943), analysing bullet-hole distributions on returning bombers: the armour belonged where there were fewest holes, because the planes hit there never came back. A real wartime memorandum — later retellings embellish it, the reasoning holds. The sales version is identical: your winning playbook was copied off the planes that made it home.
  • Contested · why rejection stings: Eisenberger, Lieberman & Williams (2003, Science) reported that social exclusion in Cyberball activates dorsal anterior cingulate cortex and anterior insula, overlapping with physical pain. The dispute has to be flagged: Woo et al. (2014, Nature Communications) used multivoxel pattern analysis to show the two are separable representations within the same regions, so the strong "social pain is physical pain" claim doesn't hold. What's safe to say: rejection genuinely recruits negatively-valenced neural responses — so the reason you keep not sending that closing note isn't laziness, it's avoiding real discomfort.

Once you have the letter, act on it: lots of C means fixing how you work the decision chain, lots of B means differentiation and pricing, lots of D means how you tell it, and lots of A — the hardest to admit — means fixing qualification, because you've been chasing people whose timing was never right. One more thing: let a single drop of "clearly this wasn't a priority for you" into the closing note and you convert someone who was merely busy into a permanent no. Close it clean, with no feeling and no hint in it.

Useful phrasing
Say it like this

"I'm closing this out on my side — no reply needed."

"If you're willing: just a letter. A timing, B someone else, C no sign-off, D we didn't make the case."

"What did you end up doing instead?"

Your Day 27 Action

Open your message list and pull out everyone who's been quiet for more than three weeks. Twenty minutes, on paper.

1 (4 min): List them all, and next to each write the reason I've invented. Read the column back — is nearly every line an explanation that involves you? That's the spotlight effect in person.

2 (3 min): Tick one box per person: not a priority / stuck internally / wrong person / genuinely not doing it. If you can't tick one, write "don't know".

3 (6 min): Send today, don't batch it. Half get tier 1, half get tier 2. Before you send, say it to yourself: I genuinely accept "not this year". If you can't, the sentence carries a hook and they can smell it.

4 (4 min): Take three that are properly dead and check one thing only: what changed on their side in the last six months? If you find a trigger, send the three-line message; if you don't, set a quarterly reminder and send nothing.

5 (3 min): Send the closing note plus one-tap reason on the deal you most recently lost, and record the letter in a two-column sheet.

One boundary: none of this rescues a deal you shouldn't have been chasing. If more than half the people on your silent list belong to accounts where you never met whoever pays, the problem isn't your re-open script — it's qualification and the decision chain. And once you've offered the one-word exit, honour it. All of that sentence's power comes from the last time you meant it.
Think It Through
1. Is "read, no reply" a different signal from no reply at all?
The difference is much smaller than you think. "Read, no reply" tells you exactly one extra thing: the message entered their field of view. It tells you nothing about what they think. The most common reality is mundane: they opened it one-handed on a commute or in a meeting, couldn't answer then, and the message permanently lost its unread marker. Plenty of read-no-replies aren't verdicts, they're a mis-tap.

So don't decode — change the action. The only reliable inference is that replying still costs more than it returns right now, so make the next one answerable in a second.

There is one signal worth taking seriously, and it has nothing to do with read receipts: replies getting shorter. A paragraph, then "ok", then nothing. The correct reading is lower the frequency and change the content — not push harder. Most people do the opposite, and convert "not a priority right now" into "I can't deal with this person".
2. They're not replying. Should I go straight to their boss?
You can, but never behind their back. Going around them reads exactly one way: you don't trust them, and you've made them look passive in front of their boss. They'll remember it for a long time, and they probably have the standing to kill you off.

What works is going around transparently, with the choice handed to them: "This probably needs finance in the room to move. Do you think it's better if I ask for a meeting with Wang directly, or would you rather raise it internally first?" Both options lead to the same place, but the decision is theirs, and most people will point the way.

There's also an easily missed diagnosis: sometimes they're silent because they can't move it either. Replying means going and asking someone else first — too much trouble, so they don't. Pushing harder only makes them hide. What they need is something forwardable verbatim: one page, three lines of conclusion, one number.

Hard boundary: if you go around, accept that you may lose that relationship for good. Whether that's worth it depends on the size of the deal, not on how frustrated you feel today.
3. Revival rates are terrible. Is it worth the time at all?
The honest part first: I can't find a credible public source for any "X% of dead deals can be revived" figure. What circulates mostly comes from sales-tool vendors' own funnels, with no control group and no way to verify it. Don't make this decision on a percentage.

Decide on cost structure instead. The version that pays: a reminder fired by a trigger, costing five minutes and three lines when it fires. It doesn't eat your best hours or your mood. Even at a few percent the expected value is positive, because a dead deal carries one enormous advantage — the discovery cost is already sunk. You know their situation, their words, their worries; on a new account that's hours of digging.

The version that doesn't: two hours every week "going through the old ones", riding the emotional swings — buying the lowest probability with your most expensive time.

So the real question isn't whether revival rates are high. It's whether you've made revival a low-cost action triggered by external events rather than a high-cost one triggered by your mood.
