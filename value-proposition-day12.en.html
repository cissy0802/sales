Day 12 · Phase C

The Value Proposition: One Sentence They See Themselves In Beats Ten Minutes About Your Product

Topic: The Value Proposition·4 principles
Asked "so what do you do?", almost everyone opens with "I" or "we." But for the first few seconds their brain is running exactly one question: what has this got to do with me? Fail that, and nothing you say afterwards gets in, however good it is.
Everything so far has been about figuring out what they want — asking, listening, digging out pain, mapping the decision chain, cutting the ones not worth chasing. From here we switch sides: it's your turn to talk. And the first thing out of your mouth isn't a product description, it's one sentence landed properly: who you help, what you solve, what result they get, and why you. Get that wrong and every demo, quote and follow-up after it is out hunting for a buyer on behalf of something blurry. Four things: swap the subject from "we" to "you," use the canvas to fill their half of the page first, run the swap test to force out real differentiation, then polish it with their own words and a five-second test until a stranger can repeat it back.
PRINCIPLE 01

The subject of the sentence is them, not you Lead with their problem, not your résumé

the four blanksself-referencethe first ten seconds
The principle in one line
A value proposition isn't an introduction to "who we are and what we do" — it's one sentence in which they see themselves. Fill four blanks and it stands up: we help [who] solve [what specific problem] and get [what result], and we're different because [why you]. Three of the four blanks are about them. The moment your first sentence starts with "we," they have to do the translating — and they won't. They'll drift.
In their words
"If your first sentence doesn't tell me who it's for and what changes for me, that's a company description, not a value proposition." — value-proposition wisdom (shared across marketing and sales)
Same thing, two openings
Scene
Setup: over dinner someone asks "so what do you do?" You're a freelance designer taking projects; across the table is the founder of a consumer brand that just closed a round.
✗ Reading the menu of yourself

"I'm a designer — mostly brand visuals, also packaging and product pages, decent range of tools."
—— Every word is about you. They have to run the translation themselves: design → brand visuals → we need new packaging → can he do it? They won't. They'll nod politely and change the subject.

✓ Swap the subject, fill the four blanks

"I help consumer companies that just raised and need to redo their brand get the new identity onto actual shelves and product pages — most people get stuck holding a pretty logo nobody knows how to use, so what I hand over in four weeks is the full set of assets, ready to ship."
—— Odds are they answer with "we're doing exactly that right now." You said nothing extra about yourself, and they claimed the problem on their own.

Why it works · mechanism & evidence
Why it works

Mechanism: the first gate on attention is "does this concern me," and information that fails it never gets processed at all. The brain runs a priority channel for self-relevant material: deeper encoding, better recall. Your first sentence either lets them insert themselves into it, or gets filed as background noise — and that gate is unconscious and closes within seconds. It isn't rudeness; their attention system already did the filtering for them.

  • Cognitive psychology · the self-reference effect (hard evidence): Rogers, Kuiper & Kirker (1977, JPSP) had people process adjectives in different ways; the group asked "does this word describe you?" recalled significantly more than those judging meaning or letter shape — a robust, repeatedly replicated effect. At the neural level, Kelley et al. (2002, J. Cognitive Neuroscience) found with fMRI that self-referential judgements specifically engage the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and posterior cingulate — the cortical-midline "self-processing" system. Putting them into the sentence is knocking on that door. (cross-ref psychology on self-reference, neuroscience on cortical midline structures)
  • Cognitive psychology · easier to read reads as truer (hard evidence): Reber & Schwarz (1999, Consciousness and Cognition) found the same statement was judged more likely to be true when printed more legibly — the more fluent the processing, the truer it feels. Oppenheimer (2006, Applied Cognitive Psychology) goes further: needlessly complex vocabulary made readers rate the author as less intelligent. So "leverage," "end-to-end," "synergies" don't earn you points, they cost you some.

Boundary: the formula is scaffolding, not a script. Don't recite the four blanks as a fill-in-the-blank tongue twister — use it to check nothing's missing, then say it like a human. And it only buys you their attention; closing still rests on the discovery, evidence and delivery that follow.

The four blanks + three words to delete
Fill it in, then say it like a person

· I help [who: specific enough that someone recognises themselves]

· solve [what: in their words, not industry jargon]

· and get [what result: imaginable, ideally with a time frame or number]

· different because [why you: mechanism / focus / proof — see next section]

· Delete on sight: leverage, end-to-end, seamless, innovative — they produce no picture at all.

English Insight
Idiomatic phrasing

"We help [who] [do what] without [the usual pain]."

WIIFM — "what's in it for me?" — the unspoken question in their head.

PRINCIPLE 02

The value proposition canvas: fill their half first Their jobs, pains and gains before your features

jobs / pains / gainsfitbreaking the curse of knowledge
The principle in one line
Osterwalder's value proposition canvas has two halves. Theirs: jobs to be done, pains, gains. Yours: products and services, pain relievers, gain creators. One rule — fill their half first, then make each line on your half point at something on theirs. Most people work backwards: list their own features, then go hunting for a customer who might care, and every line ends up matched by force.
In their words
"You don't find fit by describing your product better. You find it by describing their job, pain and gain better than they can themselves." — the core logic of the value proposition canvas (Osterwalder et al., 2014)
Fill the right column first, then point at it
Scene
Setup: you're interviewing for a growth role. Not "selling a product" — but it's the same canvas, and you're the product.
✗ Starting from your own half (listing features)

"I've done community, paid acquisition, content, I'm good with the data tools, I've led a team of three, I work hard…"
—— You read out the left column and never touched the right. The interviewer has to work out for themselves whether any of it matches the problem in front of them — and they have twenty more CVs reading their own menus.

✓ Fill the right first, then point at it line by line

"Your new product just launched, so the pressing job is getting a first batch of people who genuinely use it (job); the usual difficulty is paid acquisition is expensive and the people you buy vanish by week two (pain); the win is a thousand actives inside three months, cheaply (gain) — that's exactly the stretch I did last: zero to eight hundred-odd actives, acquisition cost down to a third of paid (kills the pain), half the content community-generated (delivers the gain)."
—— Same experience: one version is an inventory, the other an answer. Say their three boxes first, and yours have somewhere to land.

Why it works · mechanism & evidence
Why it works

Mechanism: the forced order exists to break the "curse of knowledge" — you know your own thing too well, so you unconsciously assume they know it too, and care. Once you know something, you can no longer accurately imagine not knowing it. Write your half first and every line arrives loaded with all the context in your head; what they see is a string of nouns. Filling their half first forces you to start from a position of zero information.

  • Cognitive science · the curse of knowledge (hard evidence): Newton's (1990) "tappers and listeners" study — people tapping out a song everyone knows estimated about 50% of listeners would identify it; the real rate was about 2.5% (3 out of 120). Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber (1989, JPE) showed the same in incentivised market experiments: the informed cannot strip what they know out of their judgements, and systematically overestimate how much others know.
  • Method · the value proposition canvas (a framework, not an experiment): Osterwalder, Pigneur, Bernarda & Smith, Value Proposition Design (2014). It isn't a theory validated by controlled trials, it's a worksheet that forces an order — what does the work is "customer before product," not the boxes. Treat it as methodological consensus, not a scientific law.

Boundary: a filled canvas isn't a win, it only guarantees you're not talking to yourself. The three boxes on the right have to come from what real people actually said in real conversations — a canvas filled from imagination is just self-talk arranged more neatly. (How to ask without being misled by polite lies: the Mom Test topic. How to ask until the pain is quantified: the pain-digging topic.)

Canvas self-check (run it once it's filled)
  • For each of the three boxes on the right, can I name a real person and quote what they said?
  • Does every line on the left draw a line to something on the right? Cut the ones that don't.
  • Which pain on the right is most expensive (money / time / risk)? That's the one to lead with.
  • Have I forced in "my favourite feature" when it only matches my own pride?

Idiomatic phrasing

"What job are they hiring this for — and what pain does it kill?"

"Does every line on my side point back to a pain or a gain?"

Fill their half of the page first.

PRINCIPLE 03

The swap test: put a competitor's name in — does it still read true? Focus, mechanism, proof — the only three that hold up

three sources of differenceindistinguishable = postponeddiffer where they care
The principle in one line
"We're professional, high quality, great service, good value" — swap in any competitor's name and the sentence still reads true. That's not differentiation, that's noise. Only three sources of it hold up: (1) focus — you serve one narrow group; (2) a unique mechanism — the specific how behind your result; (3) verifiable proof — numbers, cases, a guarantee. Land one of the three so a competitor can't repeat your sentence, and you're genuinely different.
In their words
"If you can swap in a competitor's name and the sentence still reads true, you haven't said anything." — positioning and differentiation wisdom (the swap test)
The swap test and the three that hold up
Scene
Setup: you do one-to-one English speaking practice. Your homepage says "experienced native tutors · authentic accent · great value." Plenty of enquiries, almost no sales.
✗ Saying things anyone could say

"Experienced, authentic, great value, fully tailored."
—— Any competitor's name fits. They open five pages and read five near-identical paragraphs, so they take the cheapest option available: don't decide. You didn't lose to a rival, you lost to "let me think about it."

✓ Focus + mechanism + proof, all three

"I only teach Chinese engineers who have to speak in English meetings (focus). We don't practise small talk — you bring the actual meeting you have next week and we drill the real agenda (mechanism). Of last cohort's 12, nine spoke up unprompted within two months (proof). If you want an IELTS score or casual chat, I'm the wrong choice."
—— That last line does the most work: someone willing to say what they don't do is believable when they say what they do.

Why it works · mechanism & evidence
Why it works

Mechanism: differentiation is really about their cost of comparison, and your biggest competitor is "not yet." When several options look alike to them, the default isn't picking the best one, it's pushing the decision back — comparing is tiring, and not deciding costs nothing today. Give them one difference nobody else can repeat and you've done the comparing for them.

  • Behavioural economics · status quo bias (hard evidence): Samuelson & Zeckhauser (1988, J. Risk and Uncertainty) showed across a series of experiments and real pension-choice data that people systematically favour the "keep things as they are" default. "Let me think about it" usually isn't dissatisfaction with you; inaction has its own gravity. (Unpacked in the buyer's brain topic.)
  • Decision psychology · hard-to-tell-apart options invite non-choice (contested — use with care): Iyengar & Lepper's (2000, JPSP) jam study is famous — the 24-flavour table drew more onlookers but only about 3% bought; the 6-flavour table about 30%. Honest caveat: Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd's (2010, J. Consumer Research) meta-analysis found the average "choice overload" effect near zero, appearing only under particular conditions. So the reliable conclusion isn't "fewer options is better" but when options are hard to tell apart and the person has no clear preference, they tend not to choose at all — differentiation is how you climb out of "hard to tell apart."

Boundary: the difference has to sit on a dimension they care about. Being unique somewhere they're indifferent to counts for nothing. So the order is always: find out what they care about most first (the questioning in the pain-digging topic), then decide which card to play. And one hard line: the proof must be real — invented numbers are the only one of the three that takes you out of the game entirely.

Pick one of the three (fill in + swap-test)
Land at least one

· Focus: "I only work with ______. I turn down everything else."

· Mechanism: "I do it differently: I start by ______, which is why ______."

· Proof: "Of the last ______ clients, ______ got ______ within ______."

· Swap-test yourself: drop your biggest rival's name into the whole paragraph — delete every sentence that survives. What's left is yours.

PRINCIPLE 04

You test a value proposition into existence — you don't think it up Their words, five seconds, one variable at a time

voice of customerthe five-second testone variable at a time
The principle in one line
Three hours of wordsmithing on your own will almost never beat one sentence lifted from a customer's mouth. Four steps: copy their words → fill the four blanks → run a five-second test → change exactly one variable and test again. Copying their words means their actual vocabulary for the pain (they said "I lose the whole weekend to reconciling accounts" — don't write "improve financial efficiency"). The five-second test: show it to someone who knows nothing about you for five seconds, then ask three questions — who's it for, what does it solve, how is it different.
In their words
"Your customers have already written your value proposition. Your job is to stop paraphrasing them into corporate mush." — voice-of-customer practice
The polishing loop and the five-second test
Scene
Setup: you're writing the headline for the first screen of a bookkeeping tool. The users are one-person business owners.
✗ Shutting the door and inventing something "elevated"

"Making financial management simpler and smarter."
—— You like it more each time you read it, because you know everything behind it. But a stranger given five seconds can't answer one of the three: who's it for? no idea. What does it solve? no idea. Different how? none at all.

✓ Copy their words → test → change one blank

Five interviewees said nearly the same thing: "the last two days of every month go on reconciling, weekends included."
Version 1: "Stop spending your weekends reconciling." Tested — "what it solves" lands, "who it's for" doesn't.
Change one blank. Version 2: "One-person business owners: stop spending your weekends reconciling." Tested again — all three repeated back. Pass.
—— You invented no words at all. You moved over what a customer already said, then added the blank they hadn't.

Why it works · mechanism & evidence
Why it works

Mechanism: your draft is contaminated by in-house jargon, while their words arrive concrete — and concrete language is easier to remember and repeat. A value proposition eventually faces a test you're not present for: they turn round and repeat it to a colleague. Abstractions leak away in that handoff almost by definition; a concrete picture travels intact. So the criterion isn't "does it sound good," it's "can a stranger repeat it."

  • Cognitive psychology · the concreteness effect (hard evidence): Paivio's dual-coding theory (1971) and the Paivio, Yuille & Madigan (1968) noun concreteness norms show that picture-evoking concrete words are remembered significantly better and processed faster than abstract ones — they run down both a verbal and an imagery channel. It's one of the most stable effects in memory research. "No more weekends reconciling" grows a picture; "improve financial efficiency" doesn't. (cross-ref psychology on concreteness)
  • Industry practice · the five-second test (weak evidence, but almost free): brief exposure followed by a recall check is standard in usability work (the Krug, Don't Make Me Think lineage). It is not a method validated by controlled trials — it's craft practice; but it's cheap enough that there's no reason to skip it, and the failure signal is unmistakable.

Boundary: copying their words doesn't mean copying every word — copy the felt pain and the specific scene, not one person's verbal tic, and get enough samples: only a phrasing that recurs across five people earns a place on your first screen. Also, the five-second test measures whether you said it clearly, not whether it sells. Clear but unwanted is a problem with the value itself, and no amount of copywriting fixes that.

One round you can run this week (checklist)
  • Pull up your last 5 customer conversations and copy their exact sentences about the pain. Don't tidy them.
  • Which phrasing recurs? Make that the skeleton of version one.
  • Send it to 3 people who know nothing about you, give them 5 seconds, ask the three questions.
  • Whichever they can't answer, change only that blank, then send it to 3 different people — keep every version.

Your Day 12 Action

Write your own sentence today — selling a product, job hunting, pitching a partnership, building a personal page, all count.

Step 1 · Copy their words: go through your last 5 real conversations and copy the exact sentences they used about the pain. Don't beautify a single word.

Step 2 · Fill the four blanks: help [who], solve [what specific problem], get [what result], different because [why you]. For the fourth, pick one of focus / mechanism / proof.

Step 3 · Swap test: drop your biggest rival's name into the paragraph and delete every sentence that still reads true.

Step 4 · Five-second test: send it to 3 people who don't know you, give them 5 seconds, then ask: who's it for? what does it solve? how is it different? Whichever they can't answer, change only that blank, and test on 3 more.

Boundary reminder: what the sentence promises has to be something you can actually deliver. Writing it louder than you can perform isn't a marketing technique, it just postpones the disappointment — and in any business that needs repeat purchase and referrals, that interest rate is steep.
Think It Through
1. My product serves several kinds of customer. How can one sentence possibly cover them?
It shouldn't cover everyone. A value proposition comes in versions per audience, not one line for all — the same product should have a different sentence on the page aimed at designers and the page aimed at finance. Do this: write version one for the group that most resembles your ideal customer profile (how to work that profile backwards is in the qualifying topic), then write separate ones for the rest. The sentence built to cover everybody inevitably dilutes into "helping organisations across industries improve efficiency," which lands with nobody. The counterintuitive part: the more specific version is the one people who don't quite match will claim for themselves — "I'm not a one-person business, but I do lose weekends to reconciling too." Concrete evokes a picture, and a picture is what makes people recognise themselves.
2. I'm new / tiny and genuinely have nothing unique. How am I supposed to differentiate?
Differentiation isn't a technical moat, so don't start there. The three easiest to get hold of are still: focus — serve one narrow group, precisely the kind a big company won't bother with; mechanism — make your how specific, since anyone can say "I do consulting" but almost nobody will say "I spend the first two weeks inside your shop watching the real workflow before proposing anything"; proof — even 3 cases, written with names, specifics and numbers, beat "we've served many clients." For beginners the truest differentiation is often service density and response speed: you can reply same-day, sit with them while they set it up, change your process for one client. That isn't spin on a weakness, it's something a large company structurally cannot do. And one that gets overlooked: honestly saying who you're not for is itself differentiation.
3. Is a value proposition the same as a slogan? Can I write something with the polish the big brands have?
Not the same thing, and this is where beginners crash. A slogan chases memorable and good-sounding; a value proposition chases clear. "Just do it" works because decades of exposure behind it already told everyone this company sells shoes — an abstract slogan invokes existing awareness, it doesn't build it. You don't have that precondition, so writing something equally abstract produces "I finished reading and still don't know what you sell." The right order is: first a value proposition a stranger can repeat; once you're known to everyone, you've earned an abstract slogan. The test is simple — run yours through a five-second test, and if all three questions come back blank, it's still a slogan. Notice too that plenty of large companies keep the plain-language version around; they just park it on the line beneath the slogan.
4. If it's all about what they want, should I never mention the value I'm genuinely good at that they haven't noticed yet?
Mention it — but not in that order. Use the pain they already acknowledge to establish relevance first; once they're willing to listen, hand them the part they hadn't noticed as an insight. Reversed — opening with "what you actually need is…" — it sounds like arrogance before trust exists, and their first reaction is defence, not curiosity. One test decides it: can that "value they haven't noticed" ultimately land back on a result they genuinely care about? If yes, it's an insight (how to teach it gracefully gets unpacked in the challenger selling topic); if no, it's your own enthusiasm. A safe move in practice: put it in the second sentence, not the first — the first uses their words to open the door, the second adds "worth mentioning, most people only discover at this stage that the expensive part is actually…". Same content, different position, and it goes from presumptuous to valuable.
