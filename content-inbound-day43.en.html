Day 43 · Phase G

Inbound doesn't sell content. It sells the conclusion he reaches himself.

Letting them come to you · educational content · nurturing · search and audience doors·4 moves · 4 diagrams
Outreach chases the small slice who are buying today. Inbound holds a place for everyone else, until the day they walk up on their own.
Content has to clear four gates: pick the thing he's actually stuck on, get the conclusion out of his mouth, survive the long stretch while he still isn't buying, and leave a road he can walk down himself. One move per gate. One, your topics are in your message history, not in a keyword tool. Two, give the whole method away — a conclusion someone reaches himself doesn't get discounted. Three, nurturing doesn't ripen anyone, it keeps you on his shortlist. Four, the door is an absurdly specific question — volume and closeness to a sale almost always run opposite.
MOVE 01

Your topic isn't in a keyword tool. It's the paragraph you've repeated three times this month. Repetition is the signal — it's already been tested on real humans.

repetition as signalcurse of knowledgeuse his words
The principle in one line
Don't start with "what does anyone search for" — start with your own message history: how many times this month did you explain the same paragraph to different people? Anything you've said three times or more is an article. The reason isn't mysterious: that paragraph has already been tested on real humans, you watched it land — and every time you deliver it live you pay full price again, so writing it down buys out the repetition once and for all. The hard part is that you'll think it's too basic to be worth writing. That judgment is almost always wrong, because once you know the answer you can no longer estimate how much someone who doesn't know it is missing.
Same paragraph: explained live vs written once
A worked example
Say you do bookkeeping for very small businesses, and five owners have asked you the same thing in three weeks.
✗ Picking the topic backwards from volume

You look it up, find that "bookkeeping services" has the most volume, and write "Bookkeeping Services: A Complete Guide." Standing under that phrase are competitors, students and people looking for a job. The ones who'd actually pay don't search that way — they search for the thing that has them stuck today.

✓ Picking it backwards from repeated words

All five asked the same sentence: "sole trader or limited company — which is actually cheaper for me?" So you write "Under £8k a month: sole trader or limited company, worked out in one table," listing tax, contributions and the cost of closing down on both sides. Only someone in the middle of that decision types that sentence — and someone in the middle of that decision is someone about to buy.

Steal this · four steps to this month's topic

1. Read your last ten real conversations (sales calls, chat threads, support tickets) and copy the other person's words verbatim — don't tidy them into business language. Their phrasing is the headline.

2. Count frequency: anything that came up three times or more gets its own piece; one-offs go in a pile for later.

3. The more it feels too obvious to write, the more you should write it. Your "too basic" is his "finally, someone explaining it in plain words."

4. Don't translate the title into jargon. He isn't searching "entity structure optimisation," he's searching "sole trader or limited company." Jargon speaks to peers, and peers don't buy.

Why it works · mechanism and evidence
Why it works

Two layers. One is leverage: explaining costs you linearly, while writing is one fixed cost against a near-zero marginal one — what content actually replaces is your repeated labour, not advertising. The other is correction: you will systematically pick the wrong topics, because you can't get back to not knowing. That bias is the curse of knowledge, and counting frequency is how you take the choice out of your own intuition's hands.

  • Hard evidence (experimental economics) · the curse of knowledge: Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber (1989, Journal of Political Economy) showed in market experiments that people holding inside information cannot discount it when predicting how uninformed others will judge — they systematically overestimate what the other side knows. Peer-reviewed, cleanly designed.
  • Moderate strength · the tappers study: Newton (1990, Stanford doctoral dissertation) had people tap out the rhythm of a familiar song; tappers predicted listeners would identify about 50% of them, and the actual rate was about 2.5%. Widely cited (it's where the Heath brothers' Made to Stick takes it from), but it's a single study in an unpublished dissertation — treat it as an illustration of the intuition, not as an effect size.

Boundaries: these two say you'll pick the wrong topic, not that a question asked three times is definitely worth writing — frequency is a cheap filter, not a guarantee. There's also survivorship bias baked in: your sample is people who already found you, so every so often you have to go ask outside the list (how to ask without being misled kindly: the Mom Test piece).

MOVE 02

Give the whole method away, because a conclusion he reached himself doesn't get discounted Self-generated conclusions arrive at full value, and stay.

self-persuasiongeneration effectdon't hide step three
The principle in one line
Educational content isn't there to inform him; it's there to get the conclusion out of his own mouth. Write "you should hire someone for this" and the sentence has to clear a defence first — he knows you sell that, so anything favourable to you gets discounted on the way in. Write "here's how to do it yourself: step one, step two, and these three parts eat the most time," and he runs it in his head, and the conclusion he arrives at is his own: undiscounted, and better remembered. Which is why the method has to be complete. A half-hidden tutorial is the worst version of all — it neither helps anyone nor hides that you're fishing.
One conclusion, two routes
A worked example
Say you do editing for podcasts and short video, and you're writing something for prospective clients.
✗ Handing over the conclusion that suits you

"Editing your own show eats far too much time — hand it to a professional team and get 3× the efficiency." His first reaction isn't "good point," it's "you would say that." And a number like "3× the efficiency," with no source, wipes out the small amount of trust he'd just extended you.

✓ Give the whole method and let him do the sums

"Editing one podcast episode alone: my timeline, my shortcuts, and the three parts that eat the afternoon" — with the minutes written next to each step, and the "lip sync takes 40 minutes" section explained properly. Readers split two ways: some follow it and succeed, and they remember who taught them; others stop dead at the line that says 40 minutes. They reach the conclusion themselves, and it doesn't get discounted.

Steal this · four rules for educational content

1. Every step, including the one you charge for. The step you're afraid to give away is the most persuasive one you have — it proves you actually do this every day.

2. Put a time or a cost on each step. The cost is what makes him do the arithmetic; your persuasive sentence isn't.

3. Say where the traps are. "I got this wrong the first time and only worked out why on the second" beats any credential paragraph.

4. Don't pivot into a pitch at the end. One line — "if you'd rather not do it yourself, this is what I do" — is enough: the smallest possible ask, with nothing demanded in exchange. Same principle as leading with value in the cold email piece.

Why it works · mechanism and evidence
Why it works

A conclusion you generate yourself sticks harder and moves behaviour more than one you're handed. Two mechanisms stack. Generating it deepens the encoding — what you work out yourself, you remember. And it routes around reactance: when someone feels pushed toward a position, they push back instinctively, and that reflex doesn't fire against their own thought.

  • Hard evidence · the generation effect: Slamecka & Graf (1978, JEP: Human Learning and Memory) found that words participants generated themselves were remembered significantly better than words they simply read; Bertsch et al. (2007, Memory & Cognition) confirmed the effect in a meta-analysis. Classic, and meta-analytically supported.
  • Hard evidence (classic) · arguing a case moves the arguer: Janis & King (1954, Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology) had one group improvise a spoken case for a position while another merely listened to the same content; the speakers shifted their own attitudes significantly more. This is the original source for "make him say it."
  • Theoretical framework · psychological reactance: Brehm (1966) proposed that when people feel their freedom of choice is threatened, they push in the opposite direction. It's a framework plus a large downstream literature rather than a single experiment — and it's exactly what explains why your conclusion gets discounted the moment you say it.
  • Brain mechanism (handle with care): Falk et al. (2010, Journal of Neuroscience) scanned people watching sunscreen messaging and found that activity in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC, the region that handles "this is about me") predicted their actual sunscreen use over the following week — and predicted it better than their own stated intentions did. A single study with roughly 20 participants, so not settled fact; but the hint is practical: what predicts behaviour is the moment he connects the thing to himself, not whether he verbally agreed.

Boundaries: this move is safest in categories where knowing isn't doing (it needs equipment, time, a licence, someone to carry the risk). In categories where reading it is enough to do it, you really will lose some people who would have paid — that's the honest cost of the move. The test: if your entire value is knowing those three steps, what you're selling was already fragile.

MOVE 03

Nurturing doesn't ripen anyone. It keeps you among the names he can retrieve. Most of them aren't uninterested. They just haven't hit their trigger yet.

not buying ≠ not needingretrieval cueslow frequency
The principle in one line
When content doesn't produce customers, the usual reason isn't the writing — it's that he isn't buying today: the kit still works, the contract hasn't expired, nothing has burned him yet. At any moment only a small slice of a category is in a purchase decision, and everyone else isn't uninterested, they just haven't hit their trigger. So nurturing can't ripen anyone who has no need — it can only make sure that on the day he finally does show up, you're one of the two or three names he can retrieve. That fixes the shape of it: low frequency where every send is worth something, rather than high-frequency presence — get filed as noise and you're off the list entirely.
You're facing two different groups
Steal this · being retrievable on the day

1. Tie the content to a trigger situation, not to your product name. He won't suddenly think "that bookkeeping firm," he'll think "I read something about this when our filings got stuck." So put the situation in the title: "The month you move office: how not to break your filings."

2. Get an address that reaches him directly (email works). Don't settle for a platform follow — the platform can cut the line between you at any moment, and you won't even know who he was.

3. Keep frequency low and make each send do one thing: hand over something he can use today. If you can't, skip the month.

4. Make unsubscribing obvious and one click. The people who stay are worth far more than list length.

5. Never automate "no reply, so increase frequency." That evicts the people who haven't hit their trigger yet — and one of them was next year's customer.

Why it works · mechanism and evidence
Why it works

Two mechanisms. The base rate: few people are in-market at all, so most of the low conversion you're seeing is that number at work, not your copy. And memory availability: when he buys, he can only pull a couple of names out of his head, and whether yours comes out depends on whether the situation he's in matches the cue you attached to yourself.

  • Hard evidence · retrieval cues decide what comes back: Tulving & Thomson's (1973, Psychological Review) encoding specificity principle showed that recall depends on whether the cues present at retrieval match the context at encoding — being memorable isn't enough, the cue has to match. That gives a directly actionable conclusion: attach content to the situation he'll be standing in, not to the name of your category.
  • Hard evidence · consideration sets are small: Hauser & Wernerfelt (1990, Journal of Consumer Research) pulled together data across categories showing that the number of brands consumers actually consider is in the single digits, far below the number available.
  • Hard evidence (with a clear ceiling) · mere exposure: Zajonc (1968, JPSP) found that repeated exposure alone increases liking; Bornstein's (1989, Psychological Bulletin) meta-analysis confirmed the effect is real but noted that it saturates and can reverse — returns flatten after heavy exposure, and it doesn't hold for stimuli that were disliked to begin with. Showing up helps; it isn't linear, and it certainly isn't a licence to flood.
  • Weaker (an industry estimate, not an experiment) · the "95-5" figure: John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute argued that roughly 5% of B2B buyers are in-market at any moment. That number is reasoned backwards from average category purchase cycles — it's a modelling estimate, not a measured constant, and it varies enormously by category (annual replacement versus once every seven years are nothing alike). Use it as an order-of-magnitude reminder, not for your own arithmetic; work yours out from your own contract cycle instead.

Boundaries: this is the easiest idea here to hide behind when nothing is working. How to tell them apart: if your content has never produced a single real conversation, that isn't a timing problem, it's a door or an audience problem (next section). Timing problems look different — someone does come to you, and says "just looking for now, maybe next year."

MOVE 04

The door is an absurdly specific question — volume and closeness to a sale run opposite What he types has already done your qualifying for you.

search as qualifyingfirst-hand onlynever one platform
The principle in one line
Doors aren't won by fighting for popular terms; they're picked up from absurdly specific questions. What someone types carries its own qualifying: "what is X" is a person learning, "how do I fix X error" or "is A or B cheaper for me" is a person solving something right now. Volume and closeness to a sale almost always run opposite — the biggest phrase brings competitors, students and the curious all at once, while the smallest brings five people a month who are actually in pain. As for which channel, there's exactly one test: on this road, can the person you just convinced reach you directly?
One person, four ways of searching, four identities
Steal this · the door checklist

1. Write the title as the question he'd type, with a qualifier where you can (headcount, revenue, situation). "Under £8k a month" is far sharper than "small business."

2. One question per piece, and the answer inside the first three lines. Writing that circles the point doesn't even get quoted any more.

3. Put in what only you have: your own first-hand numbers, the specifics of jobs you handled, a process someone could check. Second-hand summaries are worth close to nothing now — anyone can generate one in a second.

4. Leave a direct road back to you in every piece: an email address, or a line saying "tell me which step you're stuck on." Not just a platform follow.

5. Never bet every door on one platform's recommender — its rules aren't yours to control. And change the metric: don't count traffic and rankings, count real conversations started this month. Channels change; that number doesn't expire.

Why it works · mechanism and evidence
Why it works

Mechanism one: searching is an act of self-selection. His wording gives away which stage he's at, so the precision of the door doesn't depend on your judgment — it depends on the words he typed. That's the same thing as qualifying, except he did it for you this time. Mechanism two, to use and to guard against: the more fluently something reads, the more readily it is taken to be true.

  • Hard evidence (single studies, broadly supported direction) · fluency gets misread as truth: Reber & Schwarz (1999, Consciousness and Cognition) found the same statement was judged "true" more often when printed in higher contrast and thus easier to read; McGlone & Tofighbakhsh (2000, Psychological Science) found rhyming aphorisms were rated as more accurate than non-rhyming versions with identical meaning. Both had modest samples, but the wider direction — processing fluency shifting judgment — has fairly broad downstream support.
  • Which makes this a line, too: writing clearly is a service. But part of the trust a reader gives you comes from "easy to read," not from "correct" — so the more fluent a passage of yours is, the more you owe it a second pass over its numbers and sources. Making something easy to understand is the job; letting ease of understanding stop people from checking is taking advantage.
  • Mechanism, not experiment · search terms map to buying stage: I'm recommending this on mechanism. There's no clean public experiment behind it; ad-platform conversion data supports it, but that data is self-selected and the raw samples aren't published. Testing it yourself beats any industry report: ask your last ten closed customers how they originally found you.

Boundaries and uncertainty: the share of result pages that answer directly has been rising, which makes "rank first, then get clicked" shakier than it was — that's my read of the current product landscape, not a research finding, so don't take it as fact. Only one inference is safe: the more anyone can generate a piece of content, the less it's worth, and the more first-hand information is yours alone, the more likely it is to be cited.

Your Day 43 Action

90 minutes to swap "what should I write about" for "one thing already tested on real people, plus a road back to you."

One (20 min) · Read ten conversations: copy the other person's exact words from your last ten real conversations, mark the questions that came up three times or more, and pick one. The more it feels too obvious, the better the pick.

Two (40 min) · Write the "how to do it yourself": the complete steps, including the one you charge for; a time or a cost against each; and where the traps are. End with one line only: "if you'd rather not do it yourself, this is what I do."

Three (15 min) · Title it: his words plus a trigger situation or a qualifier. No jargon.

Four (15 min) · Build the road: set up a list that reaches people directly (email is the sturdiest), and fix your frequency in advance — once a month is fine.

A boundary: inbound settles in quarters, so don't draw conclusions from two weeks of data, and until it works your cash still comes from outbound (see the prospecting and cold email pieces).
Think It Through
1. I'm starting from zero — no audience, no traffic. How long before inbound brings a customer?
The honest answer: slowly, and it settles in quarters. Anyone promising you results in thirty days is selling a course, not an outcome.

But there's a way to make the wait cost less: write inbound as a by-product of your outreach work. What you write is the paragraph you're already repeating to people, which means the moment it's finished you can paste it into an email, send it to a prospect who's wavering, or use it as a work sample — so even if no stranger ever finds you all year, the time wasn't wasted. Write "apparently this topic has volume" pieces instead and they have no second use beyond waiting for a ranking; if the ranking doesn't come, the whole thing was a loss.

And one unwelcome truth: inbound isn't every business's channel. If your customers buy once or twice in a lifetime and don't search at all — plenty of local services work this way — your real leverage is referrals, not articles. The test is easy: ask your last ten closed customers how they found you. If not one says "I searched" or "I read something of yours," don't invest here yet.
2. If I publish the whole method, won't people just do it themselves and never hire me?
It depends how far "knowing" is from "doing" in your category.

Short distance — a template and three steps can replace you — and yes, you'll lose some work. But the bad news isn't the publishing, it's that your value was already thin: that kind of business gets replaced sooner or later by somebody else's tutorial or by a tool, and publishing only means you find out six months earlier, while there's still time to move up a layer.

Long distance — it takes time, equipment, a licence, someone to carry the risk — and publishing is pure gain. The most common reaction to a full process isn't "I could do that," it's "there are that many traps? I'd rather get someone who does this daily."

And here's the calculation people get wrong most often: the ones who follow your method successfully were never going to pay you. They aren't lost customers, they're free distribution — someone who succeeded using your method will say your name when a friend asks, and your name in someone else's mouth beats you saying it a hundred times. The cost is real, though: some people who would have paid will do it themselves. That's the price of this move, and what it buys back is reach and trust.
3. Search results answer directly now and eat the clicks. Is writing still worth it?
First, the part I'm not sure about: the shape of these doors is changing, and nobody has reliable data on how traffic splits three years from now. Anyone guaranteeing you otherwise is guessing.

Two inferences hold up better, though. One, reproducible second-hand content is losing value while first-hand specifics are gaining it. A "what is X" explainer can be generated ten thousand times over in a second, so its marginal value tends to zero; whereas "of the 40 jobs I handled, 11 got stuck at the same point, and here's the data" is something nobody else can write — which makes it both more quotable and more likely to make someone want to talk to you. Two, don't bet your doors on any single platform, search included. Keep at least one address you own outright; that's the only connection nobody else's rule change can sever.

There's also a more practical adjustment: swap the metric from traffic to "how many real conversations did this produce this month." Traffic rises and falls with platform rules; real conversations don't. On that metric, a piece that brings five people a month, two of whom write to you, beats a hundred-thousand-view piece with zero conversations. So the answer isn't to stop writing — it's to write less, and only what nobody else could write.
